House Price Prediction · Kaggle

Predicting the sale price of homes from around 80 messy, real-world property features. Our team built a reusable scikit-learn pipeline and a stacked ensemble of 7 tuned models that finished in the top 8% of the global leaderboard.

The task

Predict the final sale price of each home in the test set from its property features.

What we built

A scikit-learn preprocessing pipeline, engineered features, and a stacked ensemble of 7 Optuna-tuned models with a RidgeCV meta-model.

Result

Top 8% globally with RMSE 0.12119, up from rank #734 (RMSE 0.12603) with our best single model.

The challenge

Lots of features, not much data, and plenty of noise.

Accurate price estimates drive pricing strategy and planning, but this dataset makes them hard. Each of the 1,460 training homes, all in Ames, Iowa, is described by around 80 features, from lot size and build year to the quality of the kitchen. With so many features and so few homes, overfitting is the main risk.

Missing values

Some features are almost empty.

Several columns are missing for most homes, and many smaller gaps are spread across garage and basement features.

Skewed prices

Prices have a long tail.

A few very expensive homes stretch the price range, so we modelled log(SalePrice) instead of the raw price.

High dimensionality

About 80 features to choose from.

Many features overlap or carry little signal, so how they are combined and encoded matters.

Exploring the data

Understand the data before modelling it.

The project ran in three phases: exploring the data, tuning single models, then building an ensemble. We started with exploratory data analysis to understand distributions, trends and outliers, and to see where the data was missing.

Approach

A reusable pipeline, then a stacked ensemble.

All preprocessing lived in one scikit-learn Pipeline, so every experiment and the final submission used exactly the same steps. In the second phase we tuned single models (XGBoost, CatBoost, LightGBM and TabPFN) with Optuna; the best, XGBoost, reached rank #734. In the final phase we tuned seven models and stacked them.

InputRaw data~80 features1,460 training homes, each described by 79 property, location and quality features.
CleanPreprocessingImpute · scale · encodeFills gaps ("None" for categories, the mean for numbers), scales numbers and one-hot encodes categories, all in one scikit-learn Pipeline.
EngineerFeaturesSize · age · qualityAdds total floor area, total bathrooms, property age, yes/no amenity flags (pool, garage, fireplace…) and quality × area interactions.
ModelStacked ensemble7 tuned models → RidgeCVLightGBM, XGBoost, ElasticNet, Lasso, Ridge, KernelRidge and ExtraTrees, each tuned with Optuna (about 50 trials, 5-fold CV RMSE), combined by a RidgeCV meta-model.

Results

Top 8% out of a global field.

The final stacked model reached an RMSE of 0.12119 on the leaderboard, placing #419, in the top 8% worldwide. That was up from #734 (RMSE 0.12603) with our best single model, a tuned XGBoost, in the previous phase: combining models beat any one model on its own.

Where this applies

  • Pricing: estimating fair value for listings and offers
  • Value drivers: showing which features move price the most
  • Planning: data-driven decisions instead of gut feel

What I learned

  • Reproducible pipelines pay off. Keeping preprocessing in one Pipeline meant every experiment, and the final submission, used exactly the same steps.
  • Small gains matter near the top. Lowering RMSE by less than 0.005 (0.12603 → 0.12119) moved us from #734 to #419.
  • Accuracy vs. interpretability is a real trade-off. A stack of 7 models is hard to explain; in business settings, a slightly less accurate but explainable model can be the better choice.
